Ethos Safety: the guided-narrative workflow for professional assessment reports.

Ethos Safety's site visits used to end with a long evening of report writing back at the office. The guided-narrative profile, built specifically for their professional assessment work, moves the heavy lifting onto site. The accountable engineer still signs every report; they just stop rewriting it from scratch.

The starting point

Ethos Safety is a Dundee structural assessment firm. The assessments they produce are not a checkbox compliance form — they are professional reports that explain a building, its defects, the engineer's professional opinion, and recommended next actions. The narrative matters. The phrasing matters. The engineer's voice has to come through.

The previous process was: visit the site, take photos, write notes by hand, drive back, transcribe everything from scratch into a Word template. Each report was a fresh writing job. The slowest step was always the desk write-up the day after the visit, by which point the engineer's strongest impressions had already faded.

Why the standard Quickler profiles did not fit

Quickler had two main profiles when Ethos started looking. The open-report profile was too loose — it captured raw notes but left the narrative-building to the desk write-up. The verdict slot-fill profile was too rigid — it asked closed questions and produced a structured grid, which is wrong for professional assessment work where the engineer's reasoning is the product.

Ethos needed something in between: a profile that prompts the engineer through the building in the order an assessment is written, captures their professional commentary as voice notes against each section, and produces a structured draft narrative the engineer can edit and sign at the office.

The guided-narrative profile

The profile prompts the engineer through six sections: project context, building description, external elevation walk, internal walk, specific defects, conclusions and recommended actions. Each section is a sub-conversation. Within each, the engineer answers a short closed question (location, severity, dimension) plus a long open one captured as a voice note ("describe what you are looking at and what you think it means").

Voice notes are transcribed automatically. By the time the engineer is back at the desk, the draft report carries the engineer's own words in their own order, against the right photos, in the format the firm already issues.

What changed

The desk write-up time per report shrank by roughly two thirds. The engineer's professional opinion appears in the report in the form it was spoken on site, when it was sharpest. The accountable engineer still reviews and signs every report — Quickler does not bypass professional sign-off.

The harder shift was on the engineer's part: getting comfortable speaking the opinion into a voice note while standing in the building, rather than turning it over in the car on the way home. The trade-off was worth it.

What it looks like in practice

The engineer arrives on site. WhatsApp prompts them through the project context (client, address, scope). They walk the external elevations, photographing each and speaking their assessment of each as a voice note. The internal walk follows the same pattern. Specific defects are captured one at a time with location, dimension, severity classification, photograph and the engineer's professional reasoning. Conclusions and recommended actions are captured as the engineer walks back to the van.

The structured draft report is ready in the dashboard before the engineer is back at the office. The signing engineer reviews it, edits where needed, and signs.

Where the guided-narrative profile fits more broadly

The same profile pattern fits any professional assessment work where the engineer's reasoning is part of the product: condition surveys, retrofit assessments, dilapidations reports, fire-safety assessments, energy assessments. Anywhere the report has to read as a professional opinion rather than a tickbox.
