Structural engineer report software UK: field reporting without the app.

Most structural engineers write their site visit reports from hand-written notes at a desk, hours after the visit. The observations are less accurate. The desk time is expensive. This guide covers what a site visit report should include and how field capture changes the economics.

  • A site visit report should capture element-by-element observations, defect descriptions, and photographic evidence during the visit, not afterwards

The current state of structural engineer reporting

The standard workflow for a structural engineer's site visit report has not changed materially in twenty years. The engineer visits the site, takes photographs, makes handwritten notes on a clipboard or a notebook, and then writes the report at a desk — typing from the notes, inserting the photographs, formatting the document in Word.

For a residential inspection lasting ninety minutes on site, the desk time to produce the report is typically one to three hours. For a more complex inspection — a multi-storey building, a significant structural defect, a monitoring visit with several observation points — the desk time is longer.

That ratio is rarely examined. Engineers accept it as the cost of the job. But it is not inherent to the job. It is a product of the workflow. The time cost is partly in typing and formatting, but mostly in the mental work of reconstructing observations from shorthand notes taken in sequence during the visit and photographs taken in a different order. The report that takes two hours to write at a desk can be largely assembled during a ninety-minute visit if the capture tool is right.

What a structural engineer site visit report typically covers

The contents of a structural site visit report vary with the commission. A condition survey of a residential property has different sections to a monitoring visit for a retaining wall or an inspection following flood damage. The standard elements across most report types are:

  • Commission details: date of visit, engineer's name and professional standing, client name and address
  • Property description: construction type, age, floor area, number of storeys, any known previous works
  • Scope of inspection: what was and was not inspected, any access restrictions, limitations on the inspection
  • Structural elements: foundations, floors, walls, roof structure, drainage where visible
  • Observations: element-by-element description of condition, including defects with location, extent, and apparent cause
  • Photographic schedule: numbered photographs with captions, cross-referenced to the observations
  • Assessment: significance of defects found, structural implications, urgency of action
  • Recommendations: further investigation required, repairs recommended, monitoring frequency where applicable

The observations section is where most report-writing time is spent and where the accuracy of desk-based reconstruction is most uncertain. Defect descriptions written from notes and photographs are less precise than descriptions given out loud, standing next to the defect, at the point of observation.

Where current tools fall short

The most common tools structural engineers use to support report writing are a camera (usually the phone), a notebook or paper proforma, and Word. Some firms use dictation apps. Some use voice memos and transcribe manually. A small number use general inspection apps like iAuditor, configured with a custom template.

Paper proformas solve the problem of systematic coverage — the engineer cannot skip a structural element because it is not on the list. But they produce a handwritten document that still has to be typed up. Photos taken separately have to be matched to observations manually. The desk time is only slightly reduced.

General inspection apps on a phone are closer to the right answer but require training, an app install, and enough signal to work reliably on site. They are designed for checklist-style inspection — pass or fail, yes or no — rather than the narrative observations a structural engineer needs to record. "Crack in masonry, approx 3mm wide, stepped horizontal, at DPC level, first floor south gable wall" does not fit a checkbox.

Voice memos are genuinely useful for capturing observations in the engineer's own words. The problem is they are unstructured. The office receives a fifteen-minute recording with no clear division between structural elements. Someone still has to listen and type.

How WhatsApp-based field capture changes the workflow

Quickler runs structured inspection workflows over WhatsApp Business API. The engineer starts the workflow at the beginning of the visit. The workflow sends prompts for each element of the structure in turn — foundations, external walls, roof, internal elements, drainage. For each element, the engineer responds with a voice note, text, or photo, or replies "n/a" if the element is not applicable to this property.

Voice notes are transcribed automatically — the engineer's description of each defect becomes text without any desk work. Photos attach directly in the WhatsApp conversation, timestamped, and are matched automatically to the observation they were taken against. By the time the walkround is complete, the structured content of the report exists.

The PDF is generated from the captured responses. The office can see the report status in real time on the dashboard — which elements are complete, whether any observations have been flagged, whether the report is ready for review. The engineer does not return to the office to hand over a notebook.

The desk review step remains — the engineer reads through the generated PDF, adds professional commentary, and approves it. That step should remain. What is removed is the two hours of transcription and reconstruction that currently comes before it.

What to look for in structural report software

  • Narrative capture: structural observations are not checkboxes. The tool must handle free-text descriptions and voice notes, not just pass/fail responses
  • Photo handling: photos must be captured in sequence and matched to the observation they illustrate, automatically
  • No new login on site: if the engineer has to remember a password or install an update before starting the visit, the tool will be bypassed
  • Structured output: the tool should produce a consistent, professional PDF that follows the same structure every time, regardless of which engineer completes the visit
  • Fault and asset history: for monitoring visits and repeat inspections, the tool should show what was observed at previous visits against the same element

Frequently asked questions

What does a structural engineer site visit report typically include?

A structural engineer site visit report typically includes the date of the visit, the engineer's name and professional standing, the property address and description, the purpose and scope of the visit, element-by-element observations on condition, defect descriptions with location and extent, photographic evidence, an assessment of the significance of any defects, and recommended actions. The level of detail varies with the commission. A condition survey of a Victorian terrace has different sections to a monitoring visit for a failed retaining wall.

How long does it take to write a structural engineer site visit report?

For a typical residential inspection lasting one to two hours on site, a report written in Word from handwritten notes usually takes one to three hours of desk time. That ratio — more time writing than inspecting — is common across small consultancies. Capturing observations systematically during the visit, rather than from notes afterwards, removes most of the reconstruction step and can reduce desk time to a review-and-approve workflow measured in minutes rather than hours.
