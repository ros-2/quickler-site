Near miss reporting on construction sites UK: legal duties and how to do it.

Most near misses on construction sites go unrecorded. The duty to record them is implied by law even when formal RIDDOR reporting is not required. This guide covers what counts, what a good record looks like, and why most reporting systems fail at the point of capture.

Key takeaways
  • RIDDOR 2013 requires reporting of specific dangerous occurrences — scaffold collapse, structural failure, certain explosions and electrical incidents
  • Most near misses are not individually reportable to the HSE but must be reviewed under the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999
  • Good near miss records name the location precisely, describe what could have happened, and note immediate and contributing causes
  • Under-reporting has two root causes: fear of blame and the friction of the reporting process itself
  • Voice notes captured at the point of the incident are more accurate and more honest than forms completed later at a desk

What the law actually requires

The Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations 2013 (RIDDOR) set out which events must be formally reported to the Health and Safety Executive. For near misses specifically, RIDDOR requires reporting of "dangerous occurrences" — a defined list that includes the collapse of a scaffold over five metres in height, the unintended collapse or partial collapse of a building or structure under construction, and certain other specific events.

A worker dropping a hammer near a colleague, a vehicle reversing close to a pedestrian walkway, a load swinging beyond its designated zone — none of these are individually reportable under RIDDOR. But that does not mean they can be ignored.

The Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999 require employers to assess risks and to review and update those assessments when something changes. A near miss is new information about risk. Not recording it means the risk assessment reflects a model of the site that is no longer accurate.

Principal contractors routinely require near miss reporting from all contractors on site, independent of statutory duties. Failure to maintain records can affect future contract eligibility. In the event of a subsequent accident, the absence of near miss records is difficult to explain in court.

What counts as a near miss

There is no single statutory definition of a near miss in UK health and safety law. The HSE uses "near miss" and "dangerous occurrence" for different things. In practice, a near miss is any unplanned event that did not result in injury or damage but had the potential to do so under slightly different circumstances.

On a construction site, common examples include:

  • A tool or material falling from height into an area where workers were present moments earlier
  • A vehicle or plant item entering a restricted zone without hitting anyone
  • Temporary edge protection failing before anyone reached the edge
  • An excavation showing signs of collapse before workers re-entered
  • A worker discovering an unguarded hole or drop not shown on the site plan
  • Electrical equipment found live when it should have been isolated

The test is not whether anyone was hurt. The test is whether the event signals a condition on site that increases the probability of a future accident.

What a good near miss record includes

A near miss record is only useful if it contains enough information to act on. Entries that say "near miss — plant area, 10:30am" tell you almost nothing. The record should include:

  • Date, time, and precise location — not "south end of site" but the specific bay, floor level, or grid reference
  • What happened — a factual description of the sequence of events
  • What could have happened — the potential consequence if circumstances had been slightly different
  • People involved or present — names or contractor references, not just job roles
  • Immediate cause — the direct factor (unsecured load, failed barrier, missing signage)
  • Contributing factors — the underlying conditions (time pressure, inadequate briefing, weather)
  • Corrective action — what was done immediately and what further action is required

Photos taken at the time of the incident, before anything is moved or repaired, are worth more than any written description. They are also defensible in ways that recollections from later in the day are not.

The gap between what happened and what is written is largest when the record is completed hours after the event. Capture at the point of the incident, not at the end of the shift.

Why near misses go unreported

The best-run sites still under-report near misses. Two causes dominate. The first is cultural: workers fear that a near miss report will be read as an admission of fault, leading to disciplinary action or reduced standing with the principal contractor. The second is administrative: the reporting process is designed for the office, not for the site.

A typical near miss reporting form is a PDF or paper document. It requires the worker to stop, find the form, fill it in legibly, and submit it to someone. On a busy site, this takes ten minutes they do not feel they have. Nothing visibly changes as a result. After one or two unremarked submissions, workers stop bothering.

Both barriers are solvable. Anonymous or confidential reporting removes the fear of blame for the initial report. Low-friction capture at the point of the incident — ideally in a tool the worker already uses — removes the administrative barrier.

The most accurate near miss record is the one made while the worker is still standing next to the hazard.

Voice notes and WhatsApp for near miss capture

Workers on construction sites are not reluctant to use their phones. They use them constantly for coordination, photos, and communication. The friction is not the phone — it is the unfamiliar app or the form they have never seen before.

Frequently asked questions

Which near misses must be reported to the HSE under RIDDOR?

RIDDOR 2013 requires reporting of dangerous occurrences — defined events including scaffold collapse, unintended collapse of a building or structure, explosion, electrical short circuit causing stoppage of work for over 24 hours, and certain contact with overhead power lines. A near miss in the general sense — a tool dropped near a worker, a vehicle passing too close — is not individually reportable to the HSE, but must be recorded internally and used to update risk assessments. Check the HSE's current RIDDOR guidance for the full list of reportable dangerous occurrences, as the list is specific and detailed.

Do you have to keep near miss records on a construction site?

There is no statutory provision that names near miss records specifically. The duty flows from the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999, which require employers to review and update risk assessments when new information arises. A near miss is new information. Many principal contractors also require near miss reporting as a condition of contractors working on their sites, and the absence of records is difficult to explain if a subsequent accident occurs.

Why do workers under-report near misses?

Two reasons dominate. Fear of blame — workers worry that a near miss report will be read as an admission of fault rather than a safety signal. And admin friction — the reporting process takes ten minutes, involves an unfamiliar form, and nothing visibly changes as a result. Both are solvable. Anonymised or confidential reporting addresses the first. Low-friction capture in a tool workers already use — a voice note into a familiar channel — addresses the second.

What should a near miss report include?

A near miss report should include the date, time, and precise location; a description of what happened and what could have happened; people involved or present; the immediate cause; contributing factors; and any corrective action taken or recommended. Photos taken at the time add significant value. The record should be factual and specific — a vague entry that says "near miss at plant area" is not useful when you need to act on it six weeks later.
