Construction daily report app UK: paper vs digital in 2026.

The daily site log is the closest thing a construction project has to a contemporaneous record. It is the first document requested in a dispute and the last one most site managers think about when they are on site. This guide covers what to include, why it matters, and what tools actually work.

Key takeaways
  • No single statute requires a daily site log by name, but CDM 2015 and most standard forms of contract create an effective requirement
  • Daily logs are the primary evidence in delay and variation claims — their absence is routinely used against contractors in adjudication
  • Paper logbooks fail at retrieval: physically searching three months of entries for a specific date is slow and error-prone
  • Dedicated construction apps require training and often fail on older Android devices common on site
  • Scheduled WhatsApp workflows prompt the site manager at end-of-day and keep the record on the office dashboard without chasing

Why the daily site log matters

The daily site log is the contemporaneous record of what happened on site each day. It records who was there, what work was done, what instructions were received, what went wrong, and what conditions delayed progress. It is not a legal document in the formal sense. It becomes one the moment a dispute arises.

Under the JCT and NEC forms of contract — the most common standard forms used on UK construction projects — the contractor has obligations to notify delays, record progress, and maintain records supporting claims for additional time or money. The daily log is how those obligations are discharged in practice. A contemporaneous record of a delay event, made on the day it occurred, carries more weight in adjudication than a narrative reconstruction made six months later from memory.

CDM 2015 also requires the Principal Contractor to monitor health and safety throughout the construction phase. Daily site records — workforce, plant, observations, incidents — form part of that monitoring obligation. The construction phase health and safety plan should reference how monitoring records are kept.

Construction records should be kept for at least twelve years from practical completion on projects executed under a deed. Most standard form contracts are or should be executed as deeds. The daily log from a project completed in 2020 may still be needed in 2032.

What a daily site log should include

The minimum content for a useful daily site log is:

  • Date and weather: temperature, precipitation, wind — weather is the most common cause of delay and is frequently disputed
  • Workforce on site: by trade and subcontractor, with numbers — not "several bricklayers" but "4 bricklayers — AC Masonry Ltd"
  • Plant and equipment: what was on site and whether it was operational
  • Work completed: progress by location and element — "blockwork to first floor east elevation, grid lines 1–4, completed to window cill level"
  • Materials delivered: supplier, description, quantity, condition on arrival
  • Instructions received: from the client, contract administrator, or principal designer — who said what, in what format
  • Visitors to site: name, organisation, reason for visit
  • Delays and disruptions: cause, duration, trades affected, and whether a notice was issued
  • Health and safety observations: incidents, near misses, permit-to-work status
  • Follow-up items: anything requiring action by the office, the client, or a subcontractor

Vague entries are useless in a dispute. "Work continued as normal" tells an adjudicator nothing. "Concrete pour to ground floor slab, Zone A, completed 14:30. Two-hour delay due to late delivery by Ready-Mix Ltd — delivery note attached" is a contemporaneous record.

Paper and Excel: the honest assessment

Paper logbooks are still the most common tool on small and medium-sized construction sites in the UK. They are cheap, reliable, and require no training. The site manager writes entries at the end of each day. The logbook goes home with the site manager or stays in the site office.

Paper fails at three things. Retrieval: finding a specific date in a six-month logbook takes time and produces results that are hard to share quickly. Legibility: entries written at the end of a long day are often difficult to read and harder to type up for a formal claim. Availability: the logbook is in the site office when the contracts manager needs it at the office, and vice versa.

Excel solves the availability problem if it is stored on a shared drive. It fails on mobile — typing into a spreadsheet on a phone at the end of a day on site is slow and unreliable. Most site managers complete the Excel from memory at the end of the week, which defeats the purpose of a daily log.

Dedicated construction daily log apps

Purpose-built construction daily log apps — including tools like Fieldwire, Procore's daily log module, and others — produce well-structured records with good PDF output. They are designed for the use case. They require an account, an app install, and a degree of training.

The adoption problem is the same as with all site apps: engineers use them for two weeks and revert to paper when the app updates itself during a pour or the login fails on a bad signal day. Reviews on Capterra from UK construction workers consistently flag the same issues — slow on older phones, confusing template setup, per-user pricing that punishes smaller firms.

Procore in particular is priced for large main contractors. For a 10-person regional contractor running three sites simultaneously, the cost per log entry is hard to justify.

Frequently asked questions

Is a construction daily log legally required in the UK?

No single statute requires a daily site log by name. However, CDM 2015 requires the Principal Contractor to monitor health and safety during the construction phase, and most standard forms of contract require the contractor to maintain records of progress and resources. A contemporaneous daily log is the primary evidence in delay and variation claims. Its absence is routinely used against contractors in adjudication and arbitration. In practice, keeping a daily log is as close to a legal requirement as makes no difference.

What should a construction daily site log include?

A daily site log should include the date and weather conditions; workforce on site by trade and contractor; plant and equipment on site; work completed during the day by location; materials delivered; instructions received; visitors to site; any delays or disruptions and their cause; health and safety observations or incidents; and items requiring follow-up. The more specific the entries, the more useful the log in a dispute. "Work continued as normal" is not a useful entry.

How long should construction daily records be kept?

The Limitation Act 1980 gives a twelve-year limitation period for claims under a deed. Most standard form construction contracts are or should be executed as deeds. Daily site records should be retained for at least twelve years from practical completion. CDM 2015 health and safety records should be kept for the life of the structure. Digital records stored in the cloud are easier to retain and retrieve than paper logbooks stored in a site hut or a loft.

Can the site manager complete the daily log on their phone?

Yes. Any tool that captures the required content and produces a timestamped record is adequate. The advantage of phone-based completion is that the record is made at the end of the working day on site, not at the end of the week from memory.
