GDPR and field reports: what UK SMEs need to know.

Field inspection reports contain personal data. Client names, property addresses, photographs of people's homes — all of it falls under UK GDPR. This guide covers the practical obligations for small UK field services firms, without overstating the complexity or understating the risk of getting it wrong.

Key takeaways
  • Field inspection reports almost always contain personal data — client names, addresses, and property photographs all qualify
  • Most UK field services firms are required to register with the ICO and pay the annual data protection fee
  • Data should only be kept for as long as necessary — a documented retention policy is required, not optional
  • EU-hosted data satisfies the UK GDPR transfer restrictions; US-hosted data requires additional safeguards
  • This guide is practical orientation only — for specific compliance questions, consult the ICO's own guidance or a data protection adviser

What personal data field reports contain

UK GDPR defines personal data as any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. Field inspection reports contain personal data in several forms that are easy to overlook.

The client's name appears on the report. The property address is the address of a person's home. The engineer's name appears on the report. Where the property is occupied, photographs of the interior — showing layout, contents, security features, personal belongings — are photographs of an identifiable person's private space. In some cases, reports include the names of tenants or residents who were present during the inspection.

None of this is unusual or avoidable. It is necessary information for the purpose of the inspection. The legal basis for processing it is typically contract — the inspection is carried out under a contract with the property owner or landlord — or legitimate interests. The obligation is not to stop collecting it. The obligation is to handle it lawfully, tell people how you use it, keep it only as long as necessary, and protect it from unauthorised access.

Reports that are emailed to clients and stored on personal email accounts, or shared via unencrypted links, or kept on a shared drive with no access controls, are unlikely to meet the security obligations under UK GDPR. The obligation is proportionate to the sensitivity of the data and the size of the firm — it does not require enterprise-grade infrastructure — but "we just email the PDFs" is not a documented data management practice.

ICO registration: who needs to register

The UK data protection fee — commonly called ICO registration — is required for most organisations that process personal data. The Data Protection (Charges and Information) Regulations 2018 set out who must pay and who is exempt.

A field services firm collecting client names, addresses, and inspection photographs is almost certainly required to register. The annual fee is £40 for small organisations (fewer than 10 staff or annual turnover under £632,000) and £60 for medium organisations. Failure to register when required is a criminal offence. The ICO's own enforcement data shows that fines for non-registration are modest but the reputational effect of appearing on the enforcement register is disproportionate for a small firm.

Registration takes twenty minutes. The ICO provides a self-assessment tool on its website at ico.org.uk. The entry in the register shows the organisation's name, registration number, and a brief description of the processing activities. Quickler Ltd is registered as ICO C1910464. Check the register yourself before engaging any software vendor that handles your report data — if they are not registered, that is a signal worth noting.

Data retention: how long to keep field reports

UK GDPR's storage limitation principle requires that personal data is kept no longer than necessary for the purpose it was collected. There is no universal retention period. The right period depends on the type of inspection and the legal or contractual context.

For safety-critical inspections, other legislation may set the period. Gas safety records (CP12) must be kept for two years under the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998. Electrical inspection reports have no statutory retention period specified in the inspection regulations, but the limitation period for professional negligence claims — six years for contract, twelve years for a deed — suggests a minimum of six years. Landlords are required to provide the current EICR to new tenants, which implies keeping the current report at minimum.

For general field inspection reports, a documented retention policy is the right approach. The policy should state the categories of data held, the retention period for each category, the basis for that period, and the process for deletion. The ICO has guidance on retention on its website. The policy does not need to be long — two pages covering the main categories of data the firm holds is adequate for most small firms.

The practical risk of keeping data too long is lower than the risk of deleting it too early. If you cannot produce a six-year-old report when a dispute arises, you cannot produce it. The safer error is to retain longer than strictly necessary and document why, rather than to delete and find the record was needed.

Hosting: EU servers versus UK servers versus US servers

UK GDPR restricts transfers of personal data to countries outside the UK unless adequate protections are in place. The EU is on the UK's list of adequate countries, which means personal data can be transferred to EU-based servers freely. Data hosted in Germany, Ireland, or France by an EU-based provider satisfies the restriction without any additional paperwork.

Data hosted by a US-based vendor on US servers is a different matter. The US does not have a general adequacy decision from the UK. Transfers to the US require either standard data protection clauses, binding corporate rules, or another approved mechanism. Many US-based SaaS vendors use standard contractual clauses, but you should check the vendor's data processing agreement before assuming this is in place.

The practical point for UK SMEs is: ask your software vendor where the data is hosted and what protections are in place for transfers outside the UK. A vendor that cannot answer that question clearly is not a safe choice for data that includes personal information about your clients' homes.

Privacy notices and your clients

UK GDPR requires you to tell people how you use their personal data, at or before the point you collect it. For a field services firm, this means having a privacy notice that describes: what personal data you collect in the course of carrying out inspections; why you collect it and what your lawful basis is; how long you keep it; who you share it with; and how people can exercise their rights (access, rectification, erasure, and so on).

The privacy notice does not need to be handed to the client in person. Linking to it from your website and your terms of engagement is adequate. For domestic clients where the visit is arranged by email, including a link to the privacy notice in the appointment confirmation satisfies the notice requirement.

A privacy notice that has not been updated since 2018 and describes "GDPR" rather than "UK GDPR" is out of date. The UK retained GDPR on leaving the EU and then renamed it UK GDPR. The substantive obligations are almost identical, but the reference in your documents should be to UK GDPR.

Frequently asked questions

Does a small UK field services firm need to register with the ICO?

Most UK businesses that process personal data must pay the ICO's data protection fee. For a small field services firm collecting client names, addresses, and inspection photographs, registration is almost certainly required. The fee is £40 per year for small organisations. Failure to register when required is a criminal offence. The ICO's self-assessment tool at ico.org.uk takes twenty minutes and confirms whether registration is needed in your specific case.

How long should field inspection reports be kept under GDPR?

UK GDPR requires data to be kept only as long as necessary. For safety-critical inspections, other regulations may set the period — gas safety records must be kept for two years, for example. For other inspection types, a documented retention policy based on business need and legal limitation periods is required. In practice, keeping reports for at least six years from the date of the inspection is a reasonable starting point for most field trade reports, given the limitation periods for contract and professional negligence claims.

Does it matter whether field report data is hosted in the EU or the UK?

Yes. UK GDPR restricts transfers of personal data outside the UK unless adequate protections are in place. The EU is on the UK's adequacy list, so EU-hosted data satisfies the restriction. Data hosted by US-based vendors on US servers requires additional safeguards. Check your software vendor's data processing agreement and confirm where the data is hosted before assuming compliance. Quickler hosts all data on EU servers (Hetzner Germany), which satisfies the UK GDPR transfer restriction.

What personal data do field inspection reports typically contain?

Field inspection reports typically contain the client's name and address, the property address, the engineer's name, and photographs of the property — including the interior. All of these are personal data under UK GDPR. Photographs of a person's home, particularly interior photographs, require particular care. Your privacy notice should describe what data you collect in reports, why you collect it, how long you keep it, and who you share it with.
