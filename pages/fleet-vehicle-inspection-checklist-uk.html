Fleet vehicle inspection checklist UK: free download 2026.

A practical walkaround checklist for UK van and light commercial vehicle operators. Covers tyres, lights, fluids, bodywork, and load security — and explains the line between what the driver checks and what needs a mechanic.

Key takeaways
  • Daily walkaround checks are the driver's legal responsibility — not the mechanic's.
  • The checklist below covers everything DVSA expects a driver to inspect before moving the vehicle.
  • Planned maintenance is a separate, mechanic-led process at set intervals.
  • Defects found during a walkaround must be recorded and reported — not just noted mentally.
  • A digital record with a timestamp and driver name is more defensible than a paper form.

Daily walkaround vs planned maintenance: what's the difference

These two terms are often confused, and the confusion creates real compliance gaps.

The daily walkaround is the driver's check. It happens before the vehicle moves. It covers everything visible from outside and inside the cab without tools. Tyres, lights, mirrors, fluid levels visible through the bonnet or reservoir windows, windscreen condition, bodywork damage, and load security. It takes three to five minutes.

Planned maintenance is the mechanic's job. It happens at set intervals — typically every 6 weeks or every 10,000 miles for vans in intensive use. It covers brake wear, suspension components, steering geometry, and anything else that requires the vehicle to be on a ramp or partially disassembled.

The driver cannot check brake pad thickness by looking at the wheel arch. The mechanic cannot check that the load was secured properly at 6am. Each check serves a different purpose and neither replaces the other.

DVSA distinguishes between them. An operator who has good planned maintenance records but no daily walkaround records is still non-compliant. The vehicle may be in excellent mechanical condition and still be driven with a cracked windscreen nobody recorded.

Recording the check: A checklist completed and then discarded is better than nothing but is still not evidence. The record needs to survive. Paper: date, driver name, vehicle registration, findings, signature. Digital: same fields with an automatic timestamp. The record is what DVSA wants — not the good intention behind the check.

What happens when a defect is found

Finding a defect during the walkaround is not a failure. It is the system working correctly.

The driver must record the defect immediately — not at the end of the day, not when they get back to the yard. The record should include the vehicle registration, the date and time, the nature of the defect, and the driver's name.

A photograph is not mandatory but is strongly advisable. It removes any ambiguity about severity and provides a before/after comparison after repair.

The defect must be reported to the fleet manager or operator before the vehicle is used. Whether the vehicle is safe to use at all is a judgement the driver and operator make together. A cracked brake light is reportable but the vehicle can usually continue until repair. A tyre with a visible bulge means the vehicle should not move until the tyre is changed.

The repair must be recorded. A defect with no follow-up repair record is the most damaging thing you can show DVSA. It proves the fault was known.

Planned maintenance: what drivers don't need to check

Some items appear on generic inspection checklists that are not practically checkable by a driver on the roadside.

Brake pad thickness requires either a direct visual through the wheel or removal of the wheel. It belongs on the planned maintenance schedule, not the daily walkaround. The same applies to brake disc condition, hub bearing play, shock absorber performance, and exhaust system integrity beyond what is audible from the cab.

Planned maintenance intervals for vans depend on usage. Light use (under 15,000 miles per year) typically follows manufacturer service intervals. Intensive use — multiple drivers, high mileage, heavy loads — warrants 6-weekly inspections regardless of mileage.

The planned maintenance record is as important as the walkaround record. Both should be retained and producible on request.

A driver who does a thorough daily walkaround cannot compensate for a fleet that skips planned maintenance. Both are required.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a daily walkaround check and a planned maintenance inspection?

A daily walkaround check is the driver's responsibility, carried out before each journey. It covers visible defects: lights, tyres, fluid levels, bodywork damage, and load security. A planned maintenance inspection is carried out by a mechanic at set intervals — typically every 6 weeks for vans on high mileage — and covers components the driver cannot check without lifting the vehicle or removing panels.

Who is responsible for the daily walkaround check on a fleet van?

The driver is legally responsible for ensuring the vehicle is roadworthy before use. The operator has a duty to provide a system for reporting defects and acting on them. If the driver finds a defect and reports it but the operator takes no action, the liability shifts to the operator.

What tyre tread depth is the legal minimum for vans in the UK?

The legal minimum tread depth for vans (Category C1 vehicles) in the UK is 1.6mm across the central three-quarters of the tread width, around the full circumference of the tyre. Most fleet operators set an action threshold of 3mm to allow time for tyre replacement before reaching the legal limit.

Does a van inspection checklist need to be signed?

There is no strict legal requirement for a physical signature, but DVSA expects records to identify who carried out the check. A named driver with a timestamp — whether on paper or in a digital system — satisfies this. Anonymous checklists with no driver attribution provide little protection in an enforcement visit.
