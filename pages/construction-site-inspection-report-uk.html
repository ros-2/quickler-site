Construction site inspection report: what to include under UK law.

CDM 2015 and the Work at Height Regulations 2005 together set out what construction site inspections must cover and how the records must be kept. This guide explains the duties, lists what a standard inspection should cover, and describes what happens when records cannot be produced.

Key takeaways
  • CDM 2015 places inspection obligations on the Principal Contractor throughout the construction phase
  • Scaffolding must be inspected before first use, after any event likely to affect stability, and at intervals not exceeding seven days
  • Inspection records must be produced before the scaffold is used following an inspection and kept available on site
  • A prohibition notice can stop work immediately if records cannot be produced during an HSE visit
  • Photos with timestamps, taken during the walkround, are the most defensible evidence of what the inspector found

CDM 2015 and inspection duties

The Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 — CDM 2015 — impose duties on clients, Principal Designers, Principal Contractors, contractors, and workers. For site inspections, the key dutyholder is the Principal Contractor.

The Principal Contractor must plan, manage, monitor, and coordinate health and safety during the construction phase. This includes ensuring that a suitable site induction is given to workers, that welfare facilities are maintained, and that site safety is monitored throughout the project. Regular site inspections are an expected method of monitoring. CDM 2015 does not prescribe inspection frequency in general terms, but the requirement to monitor actively means that a Principal Contractor who has not inspected the site for several weeks is likely to struggle to demonstrate compliance.

On smaller notifiable projects, or on projects without a Principal Contractor, the single contractor carries the same obligations. On domestic projects, the client duties are transferred to the contractor.

Inspection records form part of the health and safety file that CDM 2015 requires the Principal Designer to compile and hand over to the client at the end of the project. Gaps in inspection records show up at handover and in post-accident investigations.

Scaffold inspections: what the Work at Height Regulations require

The Work at Height Regulations 2005 are more specific than CDM 2015 on scaffold inspection frequency and record format. Regulation 12 requires that any working platform from which a person could fall two metres or more must be inspected:

  • Before use
  • After any event likely to have affected its stability or load-bearing capacity — including strong wind, impact, or adjacent excavation
  • At intervals not exceeding seven days

The inspection must be carried out by a competent person. The written report must be produced before the scaffold is used following the inspection — not at the end of the week. The report must be kept available at the site for which it was produced and retained for a further three months after the construction phase ends.

Schedule 7 of the Work at Height Regulations sets out the information that must be in a scaffold inspection report. This includes: the name and address of the person on whose behalf the inspection was carried out; the location and description of the place of work inspected; the date and time of the inspection; the details of any matter identified that could give rise to a risk; the details of any action taken; the name and position of the person carrying out the inspection.

What a standard site inspection should cover

Below is a standard list of items for a general construction site safety inspection. The specific items will vary with the type of work and the stage of the project. This list is not exhaustive and does not substitute for a risk assessment.

  • Access and egress: routes clear, surfaces in good condition, adequate lighting, emergency exit routes unobstructed
  • Welfare facilities: toilets, washing facilities, rest area, drinking water, first aid
  • Housekeeping: materials stored correctly, waste removed, walkways clear
  • Edge protection: guard rails, toe boards, netting at all open edges at height
  • Scaffold: tied correctly, boards complete, access ladders secured, no overloading
  • Excavations: shored where required, edge protection present, no spoil at the edge
  • Plant and equipment: pre-use checks evidenced, no unauthorised use, banksmen present where required
  • PPE: correct PPE in use for the task, condition checked
  • Permits to work: hot work, confined space, and other permits current and signed
  • Signage: mandatory signs displayed, site rules posted at entrance
  • COSHH: hazardous substances stored correctly, COSHH assessments available
  • Fire: extinguishers present, no obstructions to escape routes, hot works area clear

Every item that is not satisfactory should be recorded with a description, a photo, and an action owner. Inspections that only record what was satisfactory are not useful documents.

What happens when records cannot be produced

The HSE has powers under the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 to enter premises, examine documents, and require production of records. If a site inspector visits and cannot be shown scaffold inspection records, the response depends on the inspector's assessment of the risk.

A prohibition notice stops specific work immediately until the matter is rectified. An improvement notice gives a deadline — typically at least 21 days. Prosecution can follow where there is evidence of deliberate disregard for the law or where a serious injury has occurred.

In civil claims following an accident, the absence of inspection records is treated as evidence that inspections were not carried out. The insurer's position is weaker. The dutyholder's position is considerably weaker. The legal costs of defending a claim with missing records routinely exceed what a year of digital inspection tools would have cost.

Note: this guide summarises the main legal requirements. It is not legal advice. For specific situations — complex projects, novel hazards, enforcement action — take advice from a competent H&S consultant or solicitor.

Digital inspection records: the practical case

The argument for digital records is not primarily about compliance. Compliance is achievable with paper. The argument is about quality and availability.

A paper scaffold inspection record completed at the end of the walkround, from memory, is a worse record than one completed item by item during the inspection. Photos attached to the record at the point of observation — with a timestamp — are more defensible than any description written afterwards.

Frequently asked questions

How often must scaffolding be inspected on a UK construction site?

Under the Work at Height Regulations 2005, scaffolding must be inspected before first use, after any event likely to have affected its stability (including high winds or impact), and at intervals not exceeding seven days. The inspection must be carried out by a competent person. A written report must be produced before the scaffold is used following the inspection and kept available on site. The report must also be retained for three months after the construction phase ends.

Who is responsible for site inspections under CDM 2015?

The Principal Contractor is responsible for managing health and safety during the construction phase, which includes ensuring site inspections are carried out and records maintained. On projects without a Principal Contractor, the contractor takes on those obligations. The Principal Designer holds responsibilities during the pre-construction phase and compiles the health and safety file.

What happens if a construction firm cannot produce inspection records?

The HSE inspector can issue a prohibition notice stopping the relevant work immediately, or an improvement notice with a deadline. In the event of an accident, missing inspection records are treated as evidence that inspections were not carried out. This weakens the dutyholder's position in both civil claims and criminal prosecution. Insurers treat missing records as a contributing factor to liability.

What is a competent person for site inspections?

The HSE's working definition is a person with sufficient training and experience or knowledge and other qualities to carry out the task properly. For scaffold inspections, this typically means a trained scaffolder with relevant experience. For general site safety inspections, the person should understand the hazards relevant to the work, be able to identify when control measures are inadequate, and know what action to take when defects are found.
